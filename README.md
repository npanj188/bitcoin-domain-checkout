Three separate things have to happen before a domain is yours: the blockchain confirms your transfer, the registrar credits the order, and the registry accepts the registration. A wallet showing "sent" only means the first one started.

## Send the exact amount, on the network you selected

Crypto checkout pages don't ask for a round number. They generate a precise amount, down to the last decimal, and that amount is how the processor matches your transfer to your order. Sending a rounded figure is the single most common way to end up in a support queue with money in limbo.

The network fee is separate. Your wallet pays it in BTC on top of the amount shown, and it never comes out of what gets credited. A €17.95 domain paid from a wallet with a €2 network fee costs you €19.95 in total, and only the first part reaches Ava.

Networks are not interchangeable. BTC goes on the Bitcoin network. Wrapped BTC on Ethereum, or BTC bridged to a Layer-2, is a different asset and will not be credited to a native Bitcoin address; those funds are gone, not delayed. The same applies to picking TRC-20 on the invoice and sending ERC-20 USDT.

If you're paying from an exchange rather than your own wallet, check whether the exchange deducts its withdrawal fee from the amount. A withdrawal of exactly the invoice amount minus a fee arrives short, and a short payment can fail to match the order. Send from a wallet you control where you can, and if you must use an exchange, add the fee on top rather than subtracting it.

## What bitcoin hides, and what it doesn't

Bitcoin hides your card number from the registrar. That's the whole of it.

The blockchain is public. Anyone who learns your wallet address can see that it paid a specific address, when, and how much. If you fund the payment from an exchange withdrawal, the exchange knows the withdrawal is yours and can tie it to your verified identity. Registrars that market crypto payments as anonymous are overstating it, and it's worth ignoring that claim wherever you see it.

WHOIS is a separate matter from payment. A privacy service replaces your name, address and phone in the public registration record, and it only works where the registry for that extension permits it. A handful of extensions don't. Hiding your registrant details does nothing about the payment trail, and the payment trail does nothing to your registrant details. They're two different records.

Email verification is not optional. ICANN requires registrars to verify the registrant's email within 15 days of registration, so one working address you actually read is mandatory, and a domain can be suspended if you don't respond. Ava's pricing page doesn't state whether WHOIS privacy is bundled with a registration, so if that matters to you, confirm it with support before paying rather than after.

And a .com bought with bitcoin is still a .com. It resolves in browsers through the DNS. It is not an ENS name, not an NFT, and not a blockchain domain; paying in BTC doesn't convert one into the other.

## Six ways people lose money buying a domain with BTC

1. **Sending on the wrong network.** WBTC or a Layer-2 bitcoin balance sent to a native BTC address cannot be recovered.
2. **Sending a different coin than the one selected.** The invoice is denominated in one asset on one chain. Anything else arrives as an unmatched deposit.
3. **Rounding the amount.** The exact decimal figure is the order identifier. Round it and the payment may not attach to your order.
4. **Resending because it "hasn't arrived".** A second transfer is a second payment, not a speed-up. Wait out the published window first.
5. **Letting the invoice expire or closing the tab.** Payment pages time out. If you close it, note the address and amount before you do, or reopen the pending order from your account rather than starting a new one.
6. **Confusing a DNS domain with a Web3 name.** .eth and .crypto are smart-contract records. A .com is not, no matter what you paid with.

One rule that outranks all six: no registrar, processor or support agent ever needs your seed phrase, private key, account password or transfer code. Anyone asking for them is stealing from you.
